# The Intuition Stack

A seven-layer model of how a situation becomes a feeling, an action, and a story. Lower layers are older, faster, and less accessible to report. Each layer has a biological anchor (see `02-biological-substrate.md`) and a set of **drivers** you can look for when explaining behavior.

```
L6  NARRATOR        "I bought it because it was a good deal"        slow, verbal, post hoc
L5  FEELING         core affect + concept + context → "excited"      the readable signal
L4  PREDICTOR       priors, expertise, familiarity, recognition      expectation edits experience
L3  SOCIAL          face read, status, similarity, mentalizing       100 ms judgments
L2  VALUATION       wanting vs liking, cues, habits, prediction error
L1  DRIVES          SEEKING FEAR RAGE LUST CARE PANIC PLAY DISGUST
L0  BODY BUDGET     hunger, fatigue, arousal, temperature, pain, hormones
```

Information mostly flows **up** (body state biases drives, drives bias valuation, valuation produces a feeling, the narrator explains the feeling). Predictions flow **down** (expectations change what the body reports and what the drives want). The narrator at the top sees only L5 and parts of L4.

---

## L0. Body budget (allostasis and interoception)

**What it is.** The brain continuously forecasts the body's energy, fluid, temperature, and threat needs and acts before deficits occur. The sensory consequence of this regulation is interoception. Its summary readout is **core affect**: valence (pleasant/unpleasant) and arousal (activated/calm).

**Drivers to look for.** Hours since last meal. Sleep debt. Caffeine, alcohol, glucose. Illness or inflammation. Ambient temperature. Pain. Menstrual phase, cortisol rhythm, time of day. Recent exercise. Any arousal source (a scary movie, a fight, a workout, a swaying bridge).

**How it shows up.** Low budget → irritability, risk aversion, preference for calorie-dense food, shorter time horizon, harsher social judgments. High arousal → amplifies whatever emotion the context supplies (misattribution). People reliably describe a body-budget state as a judgment about the world ("this meeting is pointless" when tired).

**Key evidence.** Barrett, Quigley & Hamilton 2016 (allostasis/interoception at the core); Craig 2009 (anterior insula and subjective feeling); Schachter & Singer 1962 and Dutton & Aron 1974 (arousal gets labeled by context; note arousal was never measured on the bridge).

## L1. Primal drive systems

**What it is.** Eight motivational circuits, subcortical and shared across mammals. Each biases attention, valuation, and action toward a class of outcomes.

| System | Pulls toward | Typical feeling words | Signature behaviors |
|---|---|---|---|
| SEEKING | novelty, resources, exploration | curious, eager, excited, hopeful | browsing, scrolling, "just looking", research rabbit holes |
| FEAR | safety | anxious, nervous, uneasy, "bad vibe" | avoidance, freezing, over-preparation, checking |
| RAGE | removing obstacles | frustrated, angry, indignant | confrontation, sabotage, "principled" refusals |
| LUST | mating | attracted, aroused, "chemistry" | display, grooming, risk-taking, generosity spikes |
| CARE | protecting kin/dependents | tender, protective, worried-for | nurturing, overspending on children/pets, rescuing |
| PANIC/GRIEF | reconnection after separation | lonely, abandoned, homesick, heartbroken | reaching out, clinging, comfort consumption |
| PLAY | social practice, joy | silly, fun, playful | teasing, games, banter, "wasting time" |
| DISGUST | avoiding contamination (pathogen, sexual, moral) | grossed out, repulsed, "that's wrong" | rejection of foods, people, ideas; moral condemnation |

**Drivers to look for.** Which system has been recently activated or deprived? Loneliness primes PANIC; a new environment primes SEEKING; a rival primes RAGE; a potential mate primes LUST and shifts spending toward display.

**Key evidence.** Panksepp's stimulation studies, decortication, anencephalic infants; Tybur, Lieberman & Griskevicius 2009 (three domains of disgust); Schaller's behavioral immune system; Griskevicius 2007 (mating motives raise men's conspicuous spending, raise women's public helping).

## L2. Valuation and learning

**What it is.** Three partly separable systems: **liking** (hedonic impact, opioid hotspots), **wanting** (incentive salience, dopamine, attaches to cues), and **learning** (reward prediction error updates expectations). Plus two controllers: a **habit** system that caches stimulus → response and a **goal** system that simulates outcomes. The habit system wins when its predictions have been reliable.

**Drivers to look for.**
- *Cues present.* Logos, smells, jingles, notification sounds, a friend's face, a place. What outcome has each cue historically preceded? Cue-triggered wanting (Pavlovian-instrumental transfer) can fire with zero awareness.
- *Prediction error history.* Was the last experience better or worse than expected? Surprise teaches; disappointment extinguishes.
- *Habit strength.* How many times has this exact context → action pair run? Overtrained actions become insensitive to whether the outcome is still wanted.
- *Expectation.* Price, brand, and description change experienced pleasure, not just reported pleasure.
- *Satiety.* Sensory-specific: the tenth bite of the same food is worth less; a new food resets value ("dessert stomach").
- *Effort cost and delay.* Both discount value steeply and asymmetrically.

**Key evidence.** Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997; Berridge & Robinson 1998 through Nguyen et al. 2021; Pessiglione 2007 (subliminal reward → effort); Plassmann 2008; Rolls (sensory-specific satiety); Daw, Niv & Dayan 2005; PIT reviews (Cartoni & Baldassarre 2016; Karger human PIT review).

## L3. Social evaluation

**What it is.** A parallel set of fast evaluators for other people: threat/trust from faces, status from signals, kinship from similarity, bonding from shared affect and repeated exposure, intentions from a mentalizing network.

**Drivers to look for.**
- *Face and voice.* Trust, competence, attractiveness judgments stabilize at 100 ms and longer looking mostly increases confidence.
- *Similarity.* Actual similarity r ≈ .47 and perceived similarity r ≈ .39 with attraction in lab studies; perceived similarity survives into real relationships, actual similarity does not.
- *Familiarity.* Mere exposure r ≈ .26 across 208 experiments; strongest for brief, unfamiliar stimuli. Proximity drives friendship largely through exposure.
- *Reciprocity.* Being liked is one of the strongest causes of liking.
- *Status.* Conspicuous spending raises perceived status and others' compliance; the audience matters more than the object.
- *Shared affect.* Watching another's disgust (or pain) activates your own insula; emotions are contagious at the circuit level.
- *Mentalizing.* People model others' beliefs with the TPJ/mPFC network, and systematically over-attribute behavior to character rather than situation (correspondence bias).
- *Bonding chemistry.* Oxytocin/vasopressin acting with dopamine in VTA/accumbens underlie pair bonds in voles and plausibly in humans.

**Key evidence.** Willis & Todorov 2006; Montoya, Horton & Kirchner 2008; Bornstein 1989; Festinger, Schachter & Back 1950; Wicker 2003; Saxe & Kanwisher 2003; Gilbert & Malone 1995; Blumenthal & Young 2023; Nelissen & Meijers 2011.

## L4. The predictive model

**What it is.** The brain is a hierarchical prediction machine. What you perceive is the brain's best guess, corrected by error. "Intuition" in the expert sense is a dense, well-calibrated set of priors acquired in an environment with regular structure and fast, honest feedback.

**Drivers to look for.**
- *Was the training environment kind or wicked?* Firefighting, chess, nursing: kind (fast, clear feedback) → trustworthy intuitions. Stock picking, hiring, long-horizon forecasts: wicked → confident but uncalibrated intuitions.
- *Recognition and fluency.* Recognized options feel better and safer. Easy-to-process names, fonts, and claims are judged truer.
- *Anchors and frames.* The first number or framing becomes the prediction everything else is compared to.
- *Implicit learning.* Statistical regularities absorbed without awareness (basal ganglia); the person "just knows" and cannot say why.
- *Offline replay.* Rest and sleep consolidate and re-plan; sudden "insights" often follow incubation.

**Key evidence.** Friston/Seth/Clark (predictive processing); Kahneman & Klein 2009; Hogarth 2001; Goldstein & Gigerenzer 2002; Lieberman 2000; Momennejad et al. 2018. Note: the "unconscious thought advantage" paradigm (Dijksterhuis) failed a large replication and should not be cited as support.

## L5. Feeling (affect → emotion concept)

**What it is.** Core affect (L0) plus active drives (L1) plus valuation (L2) produce a bodily state. The person then **categorizes** that state using emotion concepts learned from their culture and language, in light of the current context. The result is what they call a feeling.

**Consequences.**
- The same physiological state becomes "anxious" before an exam, "excited" before a date, "angry" after an insult. The label follows the context, not the body.
- Across 2,474 languages, emotion vocabularies are organized primarily by valence and arousal, with culture-specific groupings on top. Valence × arousal is the universal core; the rest is learned.
- People use feelings as information ("How do I feel about it?") and as a heuristic for risk ("feels dangerous" → judged dangerous and low benefit).
- Affective forecasting is biased: people overestimate how intensely and how long they will feel.

**How to use it.** When someone describes a feeling, decompose it: What is the valence? What is the arousal? What drive is the best fit? What in the context supplied the label? Is there an alternative label the same body state could have taken?

**Key evidence.** Barrett (theory of constructed emotion); Russell (circumplex); Jackson et al., Science 2019; Schwarz & Clore 1983; Slovic (affect heuristic); Wilson & Gilbert 2005.

## L6. The narrator

**What it is.** A language-based system that produces causal explanations for the person's own behavior and feelings. It has access to the feeling (L5) and to culturally available reasons, but not to L0–L3 directly. It generates fluent, confident, plausible accounts whether or not they are true.

**Signatures of narration (vs. mechanism).**
- The reason cites a socially acceptable, agentic motive ("quality", "value", "I just liked it").
- The reason would be given for the *opposite* choice too (choice blindness: reports for real and swapped choices are nearly indistinguishable in content).
- The person cannot produce a principle when pressed but the judgment does not budge (moral dumbfounding).
- The reason arrives *after* the behavior and does not predict future behavior as well as the cue/state analysis does.
- Mood colors the attribution: happy → dispositional, sad → situational.

**Key evidence.** Gazzaniga (interpreter); Nisbett & Wilson 1977; Johansson et al. 2005, 2006; Hall et al. 2010 (taste and smell choice blindness); Haidt 2001.

## Control: when does thinking intervene?

Default-interventionist dual-process theory (Evans & Stanovich 2013): Type 1 (L0–L5) supplies a default; Type 2 (working-memory-heavy, hypothetical thinking) intervenes only sometimes. The Daw/Niv/Dayan model says *when*: the system that is **more certain** takes control. Deliberation is recruited when fast systems conflict, when the situation is novel, when stakes are flagged, or when the person has been trained to distrust a particular intuition (Hogarth's "circuit breakers"). Deliberation is metabolically expensive and slow, so the default wins most of the time, which is the empirical fact this project starts from.

## The explanation protocol (question 0 plus seven)

Run these in order for any input. Record an answer, a confidence (high / medium / low), and the evidence tier for each.

0. **BASE RATE AND CONSTRAINTS.** What has this person done in this exact context before? What does the population do here (time-use, panel, and cohort data)? Can they and may they do the likely action? How strong is the situation (constraints, consequences, clarity, consistency)? Past behavior in a matching context is the strongest single predictor of behavior, and trait effects shrink as situation strength rises. The prediction must state the baseline it beats. Encode the situation with `framework/situation-schema.yaml`.
1. **STATE (L0).** What was the body budget? Hunger, sleep, arousal, substances, illness, time of day, recent exertion or stress.
2. **DRIVE (L1).** Which of the eight systems was activated or deprived? What primed it?
3. **CUES (L2).** What cues were present? What have they been paired with historically? Is there a habit in this exact context? What did the person expect, and what did the price/brand/description predict?
4. **PRIORS (L4).** What did their model predict? Was the relevant expertise built in a kind or wicked environment? What was recognized, fluent, anchored?
5. **SOCIAL (L3).** Who was present or imagined? What did the action signal? Who did it bond with or distance from? How similar/familiar was the target?
6. **ASYMMETRY (evolutionary).** Which error was historically costlier: false positive or false negative? (Mistaking a stick for a snake is cheap; the reverse is fatal. Missing a mate opportunity vs. a rejection; eating a poison vs. skipping a meal.) Bias follows the cheaper error.
7. **NARRATIVE (L6).** What reason did the person give? Which layers does it name? Which does it skip? Does the gap itself reveal the active driver (people rarely cite status, lust, loneliness, or hunger)?

**Output format.**

```
Situation:        <one sentence>
Base rate:        <what they did before here; what people like them do; situation strength>
Stated reason:    <what they said, if anything>
Reconstruction:
  1. <driver>  [layer]  confidence: H/M/L  evidence: A/B/C
  2. ...
Feeling signature: valence ±, arousal ↑/↓, approach/avoid, urge strength
Narrative gap:    <what the story omits and why>
Prediction:       <what would change the behavior; how to test>
```

**Evidence tiers.** A = replicated, mechanistic (dopamine RPE, sensory-specific satiety, 100 ms face judgments, similarity meta-analysis). B = well-supported but single-lab or correlational (Knutson purchase prediction, Plassmann price effect, oxytocin fMRI). C = contested or failed replication (somatic marker hypothesis as stated, unconscious thought advantage, overtraining habit induction in humans, LeDoux low road in humans, loss aversion as a fixed coefficient). Never present C as A.
