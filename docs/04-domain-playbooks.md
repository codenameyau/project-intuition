# Domain playbooks

Each playbook lists the layers that dominate, the drivers to check first, the usual narrative cover story, and what the evidence says. Use with the seven-question protocol in `03-framework.md`.

---

## A. Why did they buy it?

**Dominant layers.** L2 (wanting vs. price pain), L3 (status, social proof), L4 (anchors, familiarity), L1 (which drive the product serves).

**Mechanism in one line.** Purchase ≈ accumbens wanting − insula price pain + mPFC "bargain" signal, each measurable before the click (Knutson 2007), modulated by who is watching and which drive is active.

**Drivers to check, in order.**
1. *Cue-triggered wanting.* What cue preceded the urge (ad, notification, friend's post, a smell, a store layout)? Wanting can be triggered below awareness and does not require that the product will be enjoyed.
2. *Price relative to anchor.* Was there a reference price (crossed-out MSRP, a pricier option shown first)? mPFC fires to "cheaper than expected," not to "cheap."
3. *Drive served.* Status/LUST (watch, car, clothes), CARE (anything for children or pets, which is systematically overspent on), SEEKING (gadgets, courses, tools, "potential"), PANIC/GRIEF (comfort purchases when lonely), FEAR (insurance, supplements, security).
4. *Audience.* Mating motives raise men's spending on conspicuous luxuries and women's public prosociality. Conspicuous goods buy real compliance from observers. Ask: who will see this?
5. *Familiarity and social proof.* Recognized brand beats unrecognized at equal information. Reviews supply the "recognition" signal for unknown goods.
6. *Scarcity and loss frames.* Real but smaller and more variable than marketing lore claims; loss aversion coefficients range from 1 to 14 depending on domain.
7. *Body state.* Hunger raises impulsive acquisition in general, not just food. Fatigue lowers resistance to defaults.

**Usual cover story.** "Quality", "I needed it", "it was on sale", "I'd been thinking about it for a while." The story names L4 and L6 and omits L1 and L3 almost always.

**Prediction test.** Remove the audience, the anchor, or the cue and the purchase probability should fall. If it does not, you have misidentified the driver.

**Expectation effect.** The believed price changes the *experienced* pleasure (medial OFC), so post-purchase satisfaction reports confirm the purchase regardless of the real driver. Do not use satisfaction as evidence for the stated reason.

## B. Why did they want to eat that?

**Dominant layers.** L0 (energy state), L2 (cue wanting, sensory-specific satiety, habit), L1 (SEEKING, PANIC for comfort food, DISGUST for rejection), L3 (eating is social).

**Mechanism in one line.** AgRP/ghrelin set the motivational gain; OFC computes current reward value of each food given what has been eaten; cues paired with past eating trigger wanting independent of hunger; the narrator says "I was hungry" or "I deserved it."

**Drivers to check.**
1. *Homeostatic state.* Hours since eating, sleep (sleep loss raises hunger hormones), recent exertion, glucose crash after a sugary meal.
2. *Cue exposure.* Food ads, smells, a colleague's snack, time-of-day habit cues, a specific location. Cue reactivity predicts intake and weight gain.
3. *Sensory-specific satiety.* Full of dinner but wanting dessert is the OFC devaluing the eaten food while the sweet, cold, novel item retains full value. Variety (buffets) defeats satiety.
4. *Comfort/affiliation.* Loneliness, stress, and grief recruit calorie-dense, often childhood-associated foods. The PANIC/GRIEF system and opioid hotspots are involved; the food is a proxy for care.
5. *Habit.* Same context → same food, insensitive to whether it tastes good anymore (popcorn in a cinema).
6. *Disgust and learned aversion.* One bad experience creates a long-lasting aversion; pathogen-disgust sensitivity predicts neophobia.
7. *Expectation.* Labels ("artisanal", price, calorie claims) change experienced taste.
8. *Social.* Portion matching and food choice converge with companions; eating what the group eats signals belonging.

**Usual cover story.** "I was hungry", "I'd earned it", "I just felt like it", "I was being polite." Check whether actual hunger (time since food) matches.

**Prediction test.** Change the cue, the variety, or the company and intake should change even if hunger is constant.

## C. Why do they like that person (or thing)?

**Dominant layers.** L3 (face, similarity, familiarity, reciprocity), L0/L5 (arousal and its labeling), L2 (reward learning on the person), L1 (LUST, CARE, PLAY).

**Mechanism in one line.** A 100 ms face read sets the prior; similarity, exposure, and reciprocity raise it; reward regions (VTA, accumbens, caudate, mOFC) learn the person as a predictor of good states; arousal from any source gets attributed to them; oxytocin/vasopressin stabilize the bond.

**Drivers to check.**
1. *First-glance face/voice.* Trustworthy, warm, competent, attractive reads happen before any interaction and bias everything after.
2. *Perceived similarity.* Attitudes, background, humor, speech rhythm. Perceived similarity (not actual) keeps predicting attraction in real relationships. Central attitudes matter more than peripheral ones.
3. *Exposure and proximity.* Frequency of contact; neighbors and desk-mates become friends. Repeated exposure raises liking even without interaction.
4. *Reciprocity.* Did the person signal liking first? Being liked is a potent cause of liking back.
5. *Arousal context.* Were they met during fear, exercise, music, alcohol, a crisis? Arousal gets labeled as attraction (misattribution; the bridge study is suggestive but arousal was not measured).
6. *Reward history.* What good states has this person reliably preceded (laughter, relief, status, food)?
7. *Drive fit.* LUST (mate value signals), CARE (someone to protect or be protected by), PLAY (fun), SEEKING (novelty, "interesting").
8. *Shared affect.* Mirroring and emotional contagion build felt closeness quickly.
9. *Status/halo.* High-status people are judged more competent, kind, and funny than their behavior warrants.

**For things (brands, music, places).** Drop items 1 and 4; weight exposure, drive fit, reward history, and identity signaling (what owning or liking this says about me to my group).

**Usual cover story.** "They're genuine", "we just clicked", "good energy", "they get me." These are L5 labels, not causes.

**Prediction test.** Liking should track exposure count, perceived similarity manipulations, and reciprocity signals more than the stated trait.

## D. What do they mean when they describe a feeling?

**Dominant layers.** L5 (construction), L0 (body state), L1 (drive), L6 (vocabulary).

**Decomposition procedure.**
1. **Valence.** Pleasant or unpleasant?
2. **Arousal.** Activated or deactivated?
3. **Drive best fit.** Map to SEEKING/FEAR/RAGE/LUST/CARE/PANIC/PLAY/DISGUST. "Anxious" is usually FEAR with high arousal; "lonely" is PANIC/GRIEF; "bored" is SEEKING deprived; "disgusted by his behavior" is moral DISGUST.
4. **Context label.** What in the situation supplied the word? Would the same body state have a different name in another room?
5. **Body source.** Is there a plausible interoceptive origin (hunger, caffeine, poor sleep, illness, hormonal phase) that the person has attributed to the world?
6. **Information use.** Is the person using the feeling as evidence ("it feels wrong, so it is wrong")?
7. **Forecast inflation.** If describing a future feeling, apply the impact-bias discount: shorter and milder than stated.

**Common translations.**
- "Gut feeling" → a prediction from L4 whose reasons are inaccessible; trust it in proportion to how kind the training environment was.
- "Bad vibe" → fast face/voice threat read (L3) or DISGUST (L1); often accurate about the perceiver's past, not the target's character.
- "Hangry" → L0 misattributed as RAGE.
- "Chemistry" → L3 similarity + L0 arousal + L2 reward learning, labeled by LUST.
- "Off today" → L0 deficit without an identified cause.
- "Excited" and "anxious" → same body, different label; swapping the label changes performance.

## E. What were they thinking / why did they do it?

**Dominant layers.** All; this is the general case. Two added rules from social cognition.

**Rule 1: Correct for correspondence bias.** The observer's intuition over-attributes behavior to character. Force the situational layers (L0, L2 cues, L3 audience) before accepting a trait explanation.

**Rule 2: Run their stack, not yours.** Their body state, their cue history, their audience, their training environment. The mentalizing network defaults to simulating *your own* reaction in their position; the protocol exists to override that.

**Procedure.** Seven questions, then produce two or three competing reconstructions ranked by confidence, each with a prediction that would distinguish it. Flag where the person's stated reason is a narrative in the L6 sense and say which omitted layer is most likely doing the work.

**Common patterns.**
- Confident, fluent, principled explanation + resistance to counterargument → L6 narration of an L1/L3 driver (Haidt).
- "I don't know why I did that" → cue-triggered habit or wanting (L2) with no narrative available, which is more honest than most explanations.
- Abrupt change in judgment of a person after a small event → L3 fast evaluators updated; the stated reason will be the event, the cause is the prior it confirmed.
- Choice that contradicts stated values → different controllers; the habit/wanting system acted before the goal system was consulted.

## F. Why did they explain a complex concept the way they did?

Not one of the user's primary cases but the protocol applies: explanations are generated by L6 under constraints from L4 (what the explainer finds fluent and familiar) and L3 (what will raise status with this audience). Expect analogies drawn from the explainer's kind-environment expertise, omission of what they find obvious (the curse of knowledge), and framing that signals membership. To explain *for* someone, invert it: map the concept onto their L4 priors and the drive that makes them want to know.
